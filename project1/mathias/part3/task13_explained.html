Filling in the gaps

1 · The setup

In Parts 1 and 2 you simulated a Markov chain. You knew the true transition rates Q, used them to generate trajectories, and answered questions about those trajectories.

Part 3 flips the problem. Imagine you are a statistician with no access to the simulator. All you have is screening data from doctors: every patient is examined every 48 months, and the doctor writes down which state she was in at each visit. That is the entire dataset.

From those sparse snapshots, you want to recover the rate matrix Q that governed the underlying process.

The catch. If you knew the full trajectory of every patient — every jump, every sojourn time — estimating Q would be a one-liner:

qij = Nij ⁄ Si

where Nij is the total number of i → j jumps observed across all women, and Si is the total time spent in state i. But you don't have full trajectories — you have observations every 48 months. So Nij and Si are hidden.

3 · The big idea: guess, fill in, refine, repeat

The trick is to break a hard problem (estimate Q from incomplete data) into two easier problems we can alternate between:

STEP 1 — IMAGINE

Given a guess of Q, fill in the gaps

For every 48-month gap, simulate a plausible hidden trajectory that lands on the next observation. Use rejection sampling: keep simulating until the endpoint matches.

STEP 2 — COUNT

Given filled-in trajectories, re-estimate Q

Now every patient has a complete imaginary history. Count jumps Nij, total sojourn times Si. Plug into qij = Nij / Si. That's your new Q.

Each pass through these two steps gives a better Q than the last, because each filled-in trajectory is more realistic when generated under a better Q. We stop when Q stops changing meaningfully:

‖ Q(k+1) − Q(k) ‖∞ < 10−3

The infinity norm just means the largest single entry change. If no rate moved by more than 0.001 this round, we call it converged.

4 · How to fill in one gap (rejection sampling)

This is the heart of the algorithm. Say a patient was observed in state 0 at month 0 and state 1 at month 48. To imagine what happened in between:

Bridge between two observations
  1. Start at state 0 at time 0.
  2. Draw an exponential sojourn time using the current guess of Q. Maybe she stays 18 months, then jumps to state 1.
  3. Keep going until 48 months elapse. Note her state at month 48.
  4. If that state equals the next observation (state 1) — accept the trajectory, record its jumps and sojourns. If not — throw it away and try again.

Doing this for every consecutive pair of observations for every woman fills in the whole hidden history. The "rejection" keeps us honest: the trajectories we keep are exactly those consistent with what the doctor saw.

Why this is fair. If our current Q is the true one, the accepted trajectories are exactly samples from the conditional distribution P(hidden | observed). If our current Q is wrong, the trajectories are biased — but only as biased as our guess. As Q improves, the imaginary histories become more realistic, which improves Q further. That's the EM feedback loop.

We pretend we know Q, use it to imagine plausible hidden histories that match the doctor's observations, count jumps and sojourns in those imagined histories, recompute Q from the counts, and repeat until Q stops changing.
